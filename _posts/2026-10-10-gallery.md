**October 10, 2026.** *A virtual exhibition.*

In Chinese folklore, the phenomenon of guǐ dǎ qiáng (鬼打墙)—ghosts
building walls—describes when someone lost at night, in fog or
unfamiliar terrain, walks in what feels like a straight line and
returns to where they started. No wall is seen or felt; there is only
the fact of return. Two explanations exist. First, the folkloric,
where the living bump into barriers constructed by the mischevious
dead; second, the perceptual, positing that bodies deprived of
external reference points will tend to veer off the normal and walk in
circles.

Both record the invisible compulsion to recur. But circling is not always a failure of orientation; it may be closer to our default setting. Recurrence is what minds do once they have run out of data to process, and that tendency to spiral, to confabulate, to revisit and reconstitute the familiar, can be seen from our experience of dreams and hallucinations.

These rooms are built from walls. Though furnished with the ordinary cues of the gallery, you may find yourself disoriented and returning to the origin. As with the mischevious guǐ dǎ qiáng, that origin may not always be the same.
